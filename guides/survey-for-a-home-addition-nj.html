What survey does my architect need for an addition?

Your architect can't draw the site plan, and the township won't issue the permit, without a survey that shows the house, the lines, the setbacks and — for most additions — the ground elevations. Here's exactly what to order.

The short answer

For a home addition in New Jersey your architect almost always needs a topographic survey: a boundary survey of the lot with the existing house, driveway and improvements located, plus spot elevations and contours, delivered as a CAD file. It's what the site plan, grading plan and setback dimensions on the permit drawings are built from. A small one-story addition on a flat lot can sometimes get by with a boundary survey and a marked-up site plan — ask the architect and the zoning office.

Why a survey comes before the drawings

An architect designing an addition needs three things a survey provides and nothing else does: where the property lines actually are, so the addition can be dimensioned to the required setbacks; where the existing house sits relative to those lines, which is often not where the old survey or the tax map suggests; and the ground elevations around the house, so the new foundation, floor and drainage can be designed. Get the survey first and the drawings go faster and pass zoning the first time.

What's in a topographic survey for an addition

  • Boundary — the lot lines established and corners recovered or reset.
  • Improvements — house footprint, garage, driveway, walks, decks, sheds, fences, pool, with dimensions to the lines.
  • Elevations — spot shots and contours across the yard, first-floor elevation, top of foundation, and grade at the house.
  • Site features — trees over a certain size (many towns regulate removal), drainage structures, utilities where visible, easements from the record.
  • Deliverable — a sealed plan plus a CAD file (DWG) your architect drops directly into the site plan.

How close can I build to the property line?

That's a zoning question, and every town's answer is different — side setbacks of 10 feet in one township and 5 in the next, rear setbacks of 25 or 30, with variances possible when you're close. What the survey does is establish exactly where the line is so the setback can be measured to the inch. Homeowners who plan an addition off an old fence line rather than a surveyed line are the ones who end up needing a variance, or worse, tearing out a foundation.

Shore additions and flood zones

In flood zones, an addition can trigger the "substantial improvement" rule and require the whole structure to meet current flood elevation standards. The survey's floor and grade elevations, combined with an elevation certificate, tell your architect early whether that's in play. On Lavallette, Ortley Beach and Toms River bayfront lots this is the single most important thing to know before design starts.

Cost and timing

A topographic survey is more field work than a boundary survey and includes the CAD deliverable, so it costs more — how much depends on lot size, terrain and how much needs to be located. Turnaround is typically one to two weeks. Send us the address and your architect's contact and we'll coordinate the scope directly with them.

Questions

Home addition survey FAQ

Do I need a survey for a home addition?+

Yes, in practice. The township requires a site plan with the addition dimensioned to the property lines, and the architect needs a survey to draw it. A topographic survey is the standard order.

Do I need a topographic survey for an addition?+

For most additions, yes — the architect and engineer need elevations for the foundation, floor level and drainage. A small one-story addition on a flat lot may only need a boundary survey with the addition plotted; ask the architect.

What survey do I need for a building permit?+

A current survey showing the existing house and the proposed addition with setback dimensions, plus grading information if the town requires a drainage plan. That's a topographic survey.

Can a land surveyor determine my setbacks?+

We establish the property lines; the setback distances themselves come from the town's zoning ordinance. Once the lines are surveyed, the architect dimensions the addition to meet them.

What format do you deliver for the architect?+

A sealed PDF plan and a DWG CAD file with layers for boundary, improvements, contours and spot elevations, ready to drop into the site plan.

Can you locate trees on the survey?+

Yes. Many towns regulate removal of trees over a certain trunk size, and architects need them for the site plan. Tell us the town and we'll locate what the ordinance requires.

Does an addition in a flood zone need an elevation certificate?+

Often. If the addition crosses the substantial-improvement threshold, the town will want elevation documentation and the whole house may need to meet current flood standards. We can do the topo and the elevation certificate on one visit.

How long does a topographic survey take?+

Typically one to two weeks from order to CAD delivery, depending on lot size and schedule. Tell us when the architect needs it.

Architect asking for a survey?

Send us the address and their contact. We'll agree the scope with them and deliver a sealed plan and CAD file they can build the site plan on.
