Guide · Flood & elevation

Do I need an elevation certificate in NJ?

If your home is in a FEMA flood zone on the Jersey Shore, the answer is usually yes — either because someone requires it, or because it's the only document that can prove you're paying too much for flood insurance.

The short answer

You need a FEMA Elevation Certificate if you're buying, building, raising or substantially improving a home in a Special Flood Hazard Area (Zone AE, VE or A) in New Jersey and the lender, insurer or township asks for one. It isn't legally required just to own a home — but if your lowest floor sits above the Base Flood Elevation, it's the document that can cut your flood premium or remove the flood-zone designation entirely.

When an elevation certificate is required

There are four situations where someone will ask you for one:

  • Buying or refinancing in a flood zone. Many lenders and insurance agents request an Elevation Certificate (EC) to write or rate a flood policy on a home in Zone AE or VE. If the seller can't produce one, the buyer usually orders it before closing.
  • Building, raising or rebuilding. Shore towns require a finished-construction EC before issuing a Certificate of Occupancy on new or elevated homes. Ortley Beach, Seaside, Lavallette and Mantoloking have thousands of post-Sandy rebuilds that needed exactly this.
  • Substantial improvement. If a renovation crosses the township's "substantial improvement" threshold (typically 50% of the structure's value), the building department will want elevation documentation.
  • Challenging your flood zone. A LOMA or LOMR-F application to FEMA — the process that can remove your property from the mapped flood zone — requires surveyed elevations on the EC form.

When it's not required — but still worth getting

Under the NFIP's current rating system (Risk Rating 2.0), an Elevation Certificate is no longer mandatory to get a policy. That leads a lot of homeowners to assume they don't need one. The catch: without an EC, the insurer rates your home on modeled data, and if your house actually sits higher than the model assumes, you're overpaying. An EC lets your agent submit the real number. On barrier-island homes that were raised after Sandy, the difference can be hundreds to thousands of dollars a year.

What a surveyor actually does

A licensed New Jersey Professional Land Surveyor visits the property, ties into a known benchmark, and measures the elevation of the lowest floor, the lowest adjacent grade, machinery and equipment (HVAC, water heater), any attached garage, and — for crawlspaces and enclosures — the flood openings. Those numbers go onto the current FEMA form along with the flood zone, Base Flood Elevation and photographs, and the surveyor signs and seals it. The certificate is then usable by your insurer, lender and building department.

How to check if your home already has one

Start with the seller's disclosure packet or your closing file — an EC is often in there from a prior sale. Then call the township construction office; many shore towns keep finished-construction certificates on file for homes built or raised since 2013. If neither turns one up, or the one you find is on an outdated form or predates a raise or renovation, you'll need a new one.

What it costs and how long it takes

Pricing depends on the property's location, whether a benchmark is nearby, and the complexity of the structure — a raised single-family home is simpler than a multi-unit building with enclosures. Questions

Elevation certificate FAQ

How much does an elevation certificate cost in NJ?+

It varies with the property's location and complexity, and whether a survey benchmark is close by. Most residential certificates on the Ocean County shore fall in a predictable range; send us the address and we'll give you a firm quote the same day rather than a guess.

How long does an elevation certificate take?+

Usually a few days from the site visit. The field work itself takes an hour or two; the rest is tying into benchmarks, completing the FEMA form and sealing it. Tell us your closing or permit date and we'll schedule around it.

Does an elevation certificate expire?+

The certificate itself doesn't carry an expiration date, but FEMA periodically revises the form, and insurers and townships generally want the current version. A certificate also becomes outdated if the structure changes — if you raise the house, add a floor, or alter enclosures, you'll need a new one.

Can an elevation certificate lower my flood insurance?+

Yes, if your lowest floor sits above the Base Flood Elevation. The certificate gives your agent the actual measured elevation instead of a modeled estimate, and the higher above BFE you are, the more it can reduce the premium. It's also the first step toward a LOMA, which can remove the flood-zone designation altogether.

What flood zone is my house in?+

FEMA's Flood Map Service Center lets you look up any address, and most shore townships can tell you over the phone. Broadly: VE zones are coastal high-hazard (oceanfront and some bayfront), AE zones are the rest of the mapped 100-year floodplain, and shaded X is moderate risk. An elevation certificate records the zone and BFE for your specific lot.

Who orders the elevation certificate — buyer or seller?+

Either can. If the seller has a current one, it usually transfers with the sale. If not, the buyer typically orders it once the insurance agent or lender asks, and it's common to do it during the attorney-review or inspection period so the number is known before closing.

Do I need a survey or an elevation certificate?+

They're different documents. A boundary or title survey shows where your property lines and improvements are; an elevation certificate shows how high your building sits relative to the flood elevation. Many shore closings need both, and we can do them on the same site visit.

Can you do elevation certificates on Long Beach Island?+

Yes. We certify homes across LBI — Barnegat Light through Holgate — as well as the Toms River and Brick bayfront, the Point Pleasant and Manasquan area, and the rest of the Ocean and Monmouth County shore.
