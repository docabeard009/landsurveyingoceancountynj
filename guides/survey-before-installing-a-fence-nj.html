Guide · Fences & property lines

Do I need a survey before installing a fence in NJ?

New Jersey has no statewide law that requires a survey for a fence — but most townships require a survey with the fence permit, and building a fence six inches over the line is the single most common way homeowners end up in a property dispute.

The short answer

In most New Jersey towns, yes: the fence permit application asks for a copy of your property survey showing where the fence will go, and the fence must sit inside your boundary. Even where a permit isn't required, you need to know exactly where the line is before you dig. If you don't have a recent survey with the corners physically marked, hire a licensed surveyor to locate and stake the line before the fence company shows up.

What the township actually asks for

Fence rules are set at the municipal level, so Toms River, Brick, Howell and Manalapan each have their own ordinance — height limits, setbacks from the sidewalk, which side the finished face goes on. What almost all of them have in common is the permit paperwork: a copy of your survey with the proposed fence line drawn on it. Some towns accept the survey from your closing. Others, especially where a fence goes near an easement or a corner lot sight triangle, want something current.

The important point: the township is checking that the fence is on your property. They are not going to come out and find the line for you. That's what the survey is for.

Why the old survey from your closing often isn't enough

The survey you got when you bought the house shows where the lines are on paper. It does not tell you where they are on the ground today. Iron pins and monuments get buried under mulch, pulled out during landscaping, or knocked over by snowplows. Fences and hedges drift. After ten or twenty years, the paper is still accurate — but nobody standing in the yard can see the line.

If your survey is more than a few years old, or the corner markers can't be found, the right move is a corner-marking or stakeout from a licensed surveyor. We recover or reset the corners and set stakes and flagging along the line so the fence installer has something physical to build to.

What happens if you skip it

Fence disputes are the most common boundary case we get called into after the fact. A fence built over the line — even by inches — is an encroachment. Your neighbor can demand it be moved, it can show up as an exception on a future title survey when either of you sells, and moving a finished fence costs more than a survey would have. Building to a line you actually know is cheaper in every version of the story.

Which survey do you need?

  • You have a recent survey and can find the corners: you may not need us at all. Confirm the pins with your fence company and go.
  • You have a survey but the corners are missing: a corner-recovery and stakeout — we locate the boundary from the record and mark it.
  • You have no survey, or the lot has changed hands several times: a full boundary survey, which produces a new sealed plat you can use for the permit and for every project after this one.

What it costs and how long it takes

Cost depends on lot size, how good the existing record is, and whether the corners can be recovered or have to be re-established from adjoining properties. Most residential fence stakeouts are a single site visit. Use the cost calculator for a range, or send us the address and we'll quote it.

Questions

Fence survey FAQ

Can I use my old survey to install a fence?+

If it's recent, matches the current lot, and the corner markers can still be found in the ground, usually yes — most townships accept it with the permit. If the markers are gone or the survey predates a subdivision, easement or prior owner's fence, get the corners re-marked before you build.

Who marks the property line for a fence?+

A licensed New Jersey land surveyor. Fence companies install to whatever line you show them; they are not licensed to determine where the boundary is, and a good installer will ask you for stakes or pins before starting.

How close to the property line can I build a fence in NJ?+

Fences can generally be built right up to the line, but the ordinance varies by town — some require the fence to be a few inches inside your property so it can be maintained without trespassing, and corner lots have sight-triangle setbacks. Check the municipal ordinance; the survey tells you where the line is so you can meet it.

How much does a survey for a fence cost?+

Less than a full boundary survey in most cases, because a corner-marking or stakeout builds on the existing record. Lot size, record quality and whether pins can be recovered drive the price. We quote from the address.

How accurate does the survey need to be?+

A licensed surveyor locates boundaries to a small fraction of an inch relative to the record. That's the standard a fence should be built to — 'about here' is how encroachments happen.

Do I need a survey for a fence if I'm not near the neighbor?+

If the fence is well inside your lot and there's no permit requirement, you can proceed without one. The risk is in fences along shared lines, near easements, or along the street where sight-line rules apply.

My neighbor says my new fence is on their property. What now?+

Get a survey. It's the only thing that settles it. If the fence is over the line, it's better to know now than when one of you sells. See our guide on neighbor fence disputes.

Do you stake the line for the fence company?+

Yes. We set stakes and flagging along the boundary at the spacing the installer needs, and mark the corners with pins or nails, so the crew can string the fence line directly.

Putting up a fence this season?

Send the address and where the fence is going. We'll tell you whether your existing survey will do, and if not, stake the line before the installer arrives.
