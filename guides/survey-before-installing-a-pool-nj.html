Do I need a survey before installing a pool in NJ?

Yes — every New Jersey township requires a survey with an inground pool permit, and most pool contractors won't dig without one. The question is which kind, and whether the one from your closing is enough.

The short answer

Yes. New Jersey townships require a current property survey with an inground pool permit application, showing the pool, patio and equipment pad drawn in with their distances to the property lines, easements and the house. For a flat lot with a recent survey, a marked-up copy of that survey plus staked corners is often enough. For sloped or lagoon lots, or where drainage and grading are part of the permit, you'll need a topographic survey.

What the township is checking

A pool permit in New Jersey is a zoning review before it's a building review. The zoning officer wants to see, on a survey, that the pool and everything around it — decking, patio, the equipment pad, the fence — meets the town's setbacks from side and rear lines, sits outside any drainage or utility easement, and doesn't push lot coverage over the limit. In shore towns there's often a separate check for distance from a bulkhead or wetlands buffer. None of that can be verified from a tax map or a hand sketch; it has to be on a surveyed plan.

Which survey you need

  • Boundary survey with the pool drawn in. For a level inland lot in Manalapan, Howell, Jackson or Toms River with a good recent survey, this is usually the whole requirement: we confirm or re-mark the corners, plot the proposed pool from the contractor's plan, and dimension it to the lines and easements.
  • Topographic survey. When the yard slopes, when the town requires a grading and drainage plan, or when the pool engineer needs elevations to design retaining walls and set the pool elevation. This adds contours and spot elevations and is delivered as a CAD file the pool designer can build on. Most lagoon and bayfront lots in Brick and Toms River fall here because of flood-elevation and drainage requirements.
  • Stakeout. After the permit, we stake the pool corners and the property lines so the excavator digs in the right place. Cheap insurance against a very expensive mistake.

Can you use the survey from your closing?

Often, if it's recent and the lot hasn't changed. But two things trip people up. First, the pool has to be drawn on it with dimensions, which means a surveyor or the pool company's designer needs to add it. Second, if the corner pins are gone, the town may accept the paper but the excavator has no line to work from. In practice most pool projects end up with a corner-recovery visit at minimum.

Easements are the usual surprise

Drainage easements along rear lines and utility easements along side lines are common in Ocean and Monmouth County subdivisions, and pools cannot go in them. Homeowners frequently discover the easement only when the permit is rejected. A survey identifies it up front so the pool can be sited to fit — before the contractor has drawn plans and you've paid a deposit.

Cost and timing

A boundary survey with the pool plotted is a single-visit job on most residential lots. A topographic survey takes more field time and includes the CAD deliverable. Both are typically turned around within a week or two, and we coordinate directly with your pool contractor on what the township wants. Send us the address and the pool plan and we'll quote it.

Questions

Pool survey FAQ

What type of survey do I need for a pool?+

A boundary survey with the pool, decking and equipment drawn in and dimensioned to the property lines. If the lot slopes or the town requires a grading plan, a topographic survey with elevations and contours.

Do I need a topographic survey for a pool?+

Only when elevations matter — a sloped yard, retaining walls, a grading and drainage requirement, or a flood-zone lot where the town wants pool and patio elevations. Flat inland lots usually don't.

Can a surveyor mark where my pool can go?+

Yes. We plot the pool on the survey to meet the town's setbacks and avoid easements, and then stake the corners in the yard so the contractor digs exactly there.

What survey does the township require for a pool?+

A current survey showing the proposed pool with distances to all property lines, easements, the house and any bulkhead or wetlands. Some towns specify how recent the survey must be; check with the zoning office or ask us.

How long does a pool survey take?+

Most are scheduled within days and delivered within one to two weeks, including the plan your contractor submits with the permit.

Can I put a pool in a drainage easement?+

No. Easements have to stay clear. The survey shows exactly where the easement is so the pool can be sited outside it.

Does the pool contractor provide the survey?+

Contractors provide the pool plan; the survey has to come from a licensed land surveyor. Many contractors will ask you to order it or will refer you to one.

Do I need a survey for an above-ground pool?+

Usually not, unless your town treats it like a structure for setback purposes. Check the ordinance — but the property line still matters if it's going near a neighbor.

Putting in a pool next season?

Send us the address and the pool plan. We'll tell you which survey the township wants, check setbacks and easements, and stake it before the excavator arrives.
